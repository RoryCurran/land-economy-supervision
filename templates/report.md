# Report

## Question
Is there evidence of a home-market effect in the United Kingdom
## Findings
The data shows a pattern consistent with a home-market effect in the UK economy. This is shown through a model that calculates the elasticity of industry employment based on regional size. For tradable services, which can actually be sold and delivered across regions, we observe a coefficient that exceeds 1 across several industries; for instance, within the legal industry, a 1% increase in regional size roughly leads to a 1.26% rise in employment. 

Given that employment in some industries increases disproportionately to size, it is therefore possible that more densely populated regions will be exporting these services to peripheral regions. Thus, due to high demand in their home-market, Magic Circle lawyers might offer advice to businesses in Manchester; banks such as Lloyds can lend to companies based in Newcastle. 


## Interpretation and limitations

Since these findings rely on a regression model, it relies on the assumption that error terms are uncorrelated with regional size, so the independent variable is exogenous. This is unlikely to apply for our model: we might observe omitted variable bias, since the existence of major universities could drive both regional size and employment; and reverse causality, where sectoral employment drives regional size. For this reason, our model fails to demonstrate a causal relationship between the two variables, only showing a strong correlation. 

These concerns surrounding endogeneity can be remedied through identifying an instrumental variable, one which is directly correlated with regional size, and as a result, indirectly correlated with sectoral employment. Economists have used historical population (for instance, the fact that finance was historically located in London), soil fertility, and demand shocks to accomplish this.
