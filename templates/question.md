# Question

- **Question:** Do larger functional regions in Great Britain hold a more-than-proportional share of jobs in tradable, increasing-returns industries, and only a proportional share of jobs in local services? In other words, is there a home market effect in British employment?

- **Theory or mechanism:**
  - The home market effect (Krugman 1980; Lectures 3–4). With increasing returns and positive trade costs, a region with share θ > ½ of demand attracts a share λ* > θ of firms in the increasing-returns sector. The larger market is a net exporter of that sector's output. Firms concentrate production where demand is large and serve smaller markets by trade, because a fixed cost makes many small plants uneconomic.
  - Local services such as hairdressing, GP practices and primary schools are effectively untradable: they are consumed where they are produced. They should therefore scale one-for-one with their local market.
  - Dingel et al. (2026 draft; Lectures 3–4) find that the effect is stronger for rarer, more specialised services. Rarer means a higher fixed cost relative to local demand, so more is gained by concentrating production.

- **Expected pattern:** Across regions, estimate the elasticity *b* of each industry's employment with respect to region size.
  - *b* ≈ 1 for local services.
  - *b* > 1 for tradable, increasing-returns services (legal, banking, software, consultancy, advertising, biotech R&D).
  - *b* is larger the more specialised the industry. Hospitals should be above GP practices.

- **Scope:**
  - **Regions:** Great Britain, 2011 Travel to Work Areas (TTWAs). The unit is a *functional* region (Lectures 1–2: an area where internal exchange, such as commuting, predominates). Local authorities would split workplaces from residents: the City of London has a tiny population but huge employment.
  - **Industries:** all 4-digit SIC 2007 classes with at least 2,000 jobs nationally, with 13 spotlight industries.
  - **Year:** one recent BRES year. This is a cross-section, not a panel.
  - **What it can and cannot show:** this tests whether the *pattern* matches the theory. It does not identify the *cause*. *b* > 1 is also consistent with Marshallian externalities (knowledge spillovers, thick labour markets) or with skilled workers sorting into big cities. That is Martin's (1999) point that empirical work shows agglomeration but rarely tests NEG specifically. Dingel et al. get around this with demand shifters; a cross-section cannot.
  - **Measuring size:** if size is measured as total employment, the average industry has *b* ≈ 1 almost by construction. *b* > 1 then means "concentrates more than the average job". Resident population measures demand more directly and is the planned robustness check.
